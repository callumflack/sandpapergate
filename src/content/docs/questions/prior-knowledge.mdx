## The claim and its scope

Assess each person and occasion separately. Knowing the ball was reversing, suspecting alteration, knowing sandpaper was being used, and directing its use are distinct claims.

This page presents archive analysis based on the cited sources. It distinguishes what a witness says, what an institution found and what can be inferred from either.

## Arguments for the claim

### Bancroft names the bowlers and connects their feedback to his actions

He recalls conversations with Cummins, Starc, Hazlewood and Mitchell Marsh, says he used more sandpaper in response to feedback, and describes those exchanges continuing at Cape Town. This is more specific than an unnamed claim that the whole team knew.

**Limit:** He also acknowledges that knowledge of reverse swing, suspicion of alteration and knowledge of sandpaper differ. Feedback about swing alone cannot independently prove the third proposition.

### Sayers says his own awareness was wider than the punished trio

Sayers describes knowing about planned use and helping remove sandpaper, then questions how the original finding could be limited to three. If his account is accurate, at least one additional squad member had knowledge.

**Limit:** His further inference that every frontline bowler must have known is not a recollection of each person explicitly acknowledging it.

### The 2021 interview shows the original question and hesitation

In the complete published Guardian interview, McRae asks twice whether bowlers knew. Bancroft’s answers imply awareness while stopping short of naming a person or recounting a particular acknowledgment. This establishes an earlier public implication rather than one invented in 2026.

**Limit:** The interviewer’s view that others must have known is editorial framing, not Bancroft’s testimony. His qualified and hesitant answer is weaker than an explicit account of who knew what.

### Khawaja explicitly asserts wider knowledge in the original newspaper interview

The published interview attributes to Khawaja an all-of-us account and quotes his insistence that the team knew and should take responsibility together. This is affirmative evidence of his stated position, alongside the later ABC interview in which he declines to identify named bowlers.

**Limit:** Collective wording does not provide an individual account of when each person learned of sandpaper. The complete memoir chapter and any underlying contemporaneous notes are not captured. The article’s summaries of book events must not be treated as the full chapter.

## Arguments against or limiting the claim

### The four bowlers directly denied prior knowledge

The 2021 joint statement says they did not know a foreign substance had been taken onto the field until the big-screen images. Cummins repeated a personal denial in 2026.

**Limit:** These are the participants’ denials, not an independent finding that settles the later conflicting testimony. The four signatories do not include Mitchell Marsh. In his 12 November 2026 response, Marsh declines to share his account or explain the non-signature. That refusal is neither a denial nor an admission and predates Bancroft’s later detailed statement.

### Handling the ball or sharing a dressing room does not make awareness inevitable

The bowlers point to the umpires finding no visible reason to replace the ball. The ICC’s contemporary release independently records that inspection result. This challenges the argument that the alteration must have been obvious to everyone handling it. Paine’s published 2022 memoir passages separately reject the inference that everybody in a dressing room must know, saying players and staff keep things to themselves.

**Limit:** An umpire’s inability to see damage does not establish anyone’s private knowledge, and it concerns the inspected Cape Town ball rather than every earlier ball. Paine’s general objection predates the detailed 2026 allegations and does not independently refute Bancroft’s named conversations.

### Khawaja declines to confirm named bowlers’ knowledge

In the full ABC interview, Ferguson repeatedly asks about named bowlers and specifically Cummins. Khawaja declines to answer and says he deliberately avoided names, while advocating collective accountability. The interview therefore cannot be presented as a specific factual confirmation that each named bowler knew.

**Limit:** Declining to answer is not a retraction or proof of ignorance. The complete memoir chapter is still needed to assess the factual observations he asks readers to consider.

### Starc repeats a personal denial rather than supplying a different full-team account

The captured November 2026 report quotes Starc saying his recollection is unchanged and that he was unaware of a substance being taken onto the field before seeing it on the big screen. It adds a contemporaneous personal response to the 2021 joint denial.

**Limit:** The full press exchange is not captured, and the quoted denial concerns his knowledge; it cannot independently establish what others knew. The report also says he had not read the book.

### Cummins offers a different explanation of the huddle language

ABC’s live record captures the specific question about telling teammates not to do anything else to a reversing ball. Cummins says he does not remember that exchange but describes similar instructions as part of ordinary ball maintenance. This is a competing explanation for feedback that the wider-knowledge argument treats as incriminating.

**Limit:** This is his explanation of normal practice, not independent proof of what happened in that huddle or a historical ruling that every method he describes was lawful. Lack of recollection is not proof the conversation did not happen.
