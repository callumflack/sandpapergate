## What Khawaja alleges

Khawaja’s original newspaper interview expressly includes batters, bowlers and staff in his all-of-us account. The proposition tested here is that every relevant player and staff member knew about sandpaper use before its public exposure. Knowledge of reverse swing, knowledge of a proposal, knowledge of actual use and participation in cheating are distinct. Someone could know and object.

The [individual Cummins question](/questions/cummins-in-2018/) examines his evidence separately; this page compares the three witnesses across the whole group.

**Source boundary:** The complete Sabr chapter and reported 2018 notes are absent. The November 7 article mixes direct interview quotations with Brettig’s book summaries; the ABC transcript preserves Khawaja’s answers separately from Ferguson’s premises. Bancroft’s later specificity cannot be inserted into Khawaja’s account. The captured Bancroft statement is a bylined access copy, not an independently compared original; direct listening checks for the interviews remain open.

## Strongest case for the claim

### Khawaja: reported dressing-room observations, without individual bowler particulars

**What the captured sources say he saw or heard.** Brettig’s account of the book describes open Port Elizabeth discussion about roughing the ball and sandpaper, Smith distancing himself, and Bancroft volunteering. At Cape Town it places Khawaja beside Bancroft, observing an exchange with an unnamed coach. ABC’s later live blog also reports a book scene involving a dressing-room sandpaper demonstration and laughter, and quotes an unnamed coach asking Bancroft to do more. Those are attributed book accounts, not the complete chapter or independent witnesses to the scenes.

**Who he specifically identifies.** In the November 7 reported scenes, Smith and Bancroft are named; the coach is not. The captured material does not give Khawaja’s person-by-person account of Cummins, Starc, Hazlewood or Lyon acknowledging sandpaper. His broader language implicates the whole group, including himself, but it is less specific than Bancroft’s subsequent account.

**What he says directly when pressed.** Khawaja stands by what he saw and the conversations at Port Elizabeth, says he deliberately removed names, and declines to answer whether the named bowlers participated in discussions or Cummins knew. Ferguson supplies the categorical inference about Cummins; Khawaja does not expressly confirm it.

**Limit:** His broad assertion is affirmative testimony and should not be erased by the non-answer. But the non-answer supplies no additional named observation. His reported proximity could make him a useful witness to a coach’s exchange; proximity alone does not show that every nearby person heard it. The reported 2018 notes are not contemporary corroboration until their actual contents and provenance can be examined.

### Bancroft: named exchanges, an alleged open acknowledgment, and an informed instruction

**At the Port Elizabeth tea break.** Bancroft says Warner proposed sandpaper and explained why camera attention meant he could not use it himself. He puts Saker and Lehmann in the conversation, recalls people agreeing, and says Smith entered and distanced himself. He expressly qualifies this: not everyone was in the room, and he cannot speak for every word shared.

**On the field.** He admits using sandpaper and names **Cummins, Starc, Hazlewood and Mitchell Marsh** in conversations about whether the ball was reversing enough. He names **Paine** as another source of feedback, recalls more work being requested and a warning to stop, and singles out Marsh joking about the swing. At Cape Town he says exchanges continued with those four fast bowlers, Paine and unnamed slips fielders. He explicitly alleges that these teammates knew he was using sandpaper, rather than merely working the seam; that allegation must be preserved.

**The strongest alleged disclosure to a group.** He says that at the usual team chat on the morning of Port Elizabeth day three, his sandpaper use the previous evening was discussed and acknowledged. He singles out **Saker’s** encouragement and **Kate Hutchison’s** objection and departure. This is a much more direct proposed route to shared knowledge than the proposition that all bowlers must notice a ball’s condition. Hutchison is described as objecting, not approving.

**At Cape Town.** He names **Saker** as the coach who instructed him to do more with sandpaper. He also places himself beside Khawaja and Paine. His later story about **Handscomb and Lehmann’s radio instruction** is expressly after he was caught; it is evidence of alleged knowledge then, not by itself before exposure.

**Limit:** This is the most specific witness account in the three, but it is retrospective. Bancroft admits earlier withholding of material facts and says Khawaja’s book revived memories. His explicit knowledge allegation goes beyond the mostly swing-related words he recounts to bowlers; a competing innocent interpretation remains possible unless context establishes shared knowledge. The alleged day-three discussion is more probative if accurately recalled, but the archive lacks an attendance list or another comparably detailed account of that meeting. A vivid memory is not independent verification.

### Sayers: direct knowledge and handling; broader knowledge asserted partly by inference

**What he says he personally knew and recalls.** Sayers locates his first knowledge at Port Elizabeth and remembers numerous dressing-room conversations about reversing the ball and what would be used. He does not remember whose idea it was. He says he recalls Bancroft agreeing to act and explains Warner’s non-participation by camera attention; he does not supply a direct Warner quotation proving the origin of the plan.

**His physical involvement.** He says sandpaper was handed to him to take off the field while he brought drinks. He assumes Bancroft passed it, rather than distinctly recalling the handover person, and cannot remember what he did with it afterwards. If accepted, his own admitted knowledge and handling are powerful evidence of an additional informed squad member.

**Who else he identifies.** Bancroft and Warner appear in his account of the plan. He does not name a frontline bowler’s sandpaper acknowledgment or an individual coach’s instruction in this interview. Asked whether the frontline bowlers could have been unaware, he reasons that a reverse-swing effort requires everyone to buy in and concludes everyone knew. His confidence is broader than the named observations he supplies.

**Limit:** Sayers is not merely repeating Khawaja: he alleges personal knowledge and handling. But that is stronger proof about Sayers than about every other person. He does not recall discussion of the strategy’s success when asked, and believes use did not continue in that Port Elizabeth Test; Bancroft says it continued. Their agreement on onset and wider awareness is not agreement on duration or independent corroboration of Bancroft’s next-morning meeting.

## Strongest challenge or limit

### Cummins: an explicit personal denial, with an alternative reading of the feedback

**Against which allegation?** Bancroft names Cummins in feedback conversations at both Tests and says the bowlers knew the method. Khawaja’s group allegation includes him, but the captured Khawaja answers do not give a specific Cummins acknowledgment.

**The captured response.** On November 13 Cummins says he did not know sandpaper was going onto the field and that the earlier Port Elizabeth episode was news to him. He also rejects being an architect of a sandpaper scheme. On the reported dressing-room demonstration, he has no recollection but expressly does not say it never happened. On the coach’s demand for more and a bowler’s instruction to stop further ball work, he lacks specific recollection but says such feedback could happen. He offers routine ball management as an explanation for swing-related language.

**How it stacks up.** His knowledge denial directly conflicts with Bancroft’s asserted shared understanding. His alternative account does address the ambiguity of feedback about swing. It does not refute a correctly recalled open discussion naming sandpaper. Rejecting authorship of a scheme is also a stronger denial than rejecting mere awareness; proof of one would not automatically prove the other.

**After Bancroft spoke.** In the [November 19 ABC report](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-19/pat-cummins-maintains-position-on-sandpapergate/107402100), Cummins’s manager Neil Maxwell reaffirms the unchanged position and says the earlier conference answered Bancroft’s allegations. That is a response after the statement, conveyed through his manager; it does not add a new detailed recollection of each disputed exchange.

**Limit:** The November 13 conference predates Bancroft’s November 18 statement. The later endorsement through Maxwell should not be omitted, but does not turn the earlier answers into a new passage-by-passage interview. His denial is participant testimony, not independent exoneration; qualified recollections must not be upgraded into categorical denials of events.

## Where the record stands

The captured record contains direct self-implication by Sayers, Khawaja’s broad assertion and reported observations, and Bancroft’s much more specific allegations against named people.
